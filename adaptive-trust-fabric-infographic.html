Adaptive Trust Fabric

Identity · security · resilience

Trust is a decision, not a location.

An Adaptive Trust Fabric connects identity, policy, enforcement and recovery across cloud, local and operational environments—without assuming that a network or tenant boundary makes access safe.

The central question

Who can do what, to which resource, under which conditions—and what happens when the services behind that decision fail?

01 / Access

Make trust explicit

Evaluate each request against its context. Being inside a network or a tenant is not, by itself, authorization.

1

Identify

Establish the person, workload or agent, the resource and the action requested.

2

Evaluate

Consider identity assurance, device or workload state, data classification, purpose and jurisdiction.

3

Enforce

Apply the decision at the relevant identity, application, API, cloud, data or local control.

4

Observe & review

Record actions, monitor for drift and revoke or change access when conditions or authority change.

The same principle applies to non-human identities: give agents accountable sponsors, least privilege and a way to revoke their access.

02 / Architecture

Separate responsibilities

A shared policy does not require every control—or every recovery dependency—to live in the same place.

Set direction

Policy ownership

Define requirements, accountable owners, policy versions and exceptions.

Control access

Identity & enforcement

Assign authentication, authorization and key administration to the systems that implement them.

See & respond

Telemetry & operations

Collect audit records and provide detection and response where they are needed, including locally.

Restore safely

Recovery authority

Keep recovery identities, tools, keys and baselines usable when the affected environment is unavailable or compromised.

03 / Boundaries

Choose separation for a reason

Identity, administration, data, network and physical-safety boundaries solve different problems. A separate tenant alone does not guarantee independent identity or recovery.

Start with one tenant—if it meets the requirements.

Add separation when a documented identity, legal, sovereignty, safety, operational or recovery requirement cannot be met within it. Record ownership, dependencies and the cost of operating the boundary.

T1

Delegated

One tenant where shared administration and required boundaries are sufficient.

T2

Federated

Separate tenants collaborate while maintaining distinct control planes.

T3

Independent

Local authority is required for identity, keys, administration or recovery.

T4

Disconnected

An enclave or operational technology (OT) site must operate without upstream services.

T5

Transition

Restricted access supports acquisition discovery and remediation before expansion.

Tradeoff: more control planes can reduce shared exposure, but add policy, monitoring, staffing and recovery complexity. Shared identities and privileged integrations can also cross an intended boundary.

04 / Continuity

Design for the outage

Assign each environment a required survivability tier. These are operating requirements, not guarantees that a product or site meets them.

S0

Cloud-dependent

Normal online operation; central identity, policy and security operations.

S1

Transient outage

Required functions continue for hours using supported session continuity and emergency access.

S2

Extended degraded

Days of reduced operation with selected local authorization, telemetry and recovery resources.

S3

Independent operation

Required functions continue without upstream control planes, including local detection and response.

S4

Disconnected

Intentional isolation with local identity, offline policy distribution, monitoring and recovery.

Example / S3 site

What if the connection to central services fails?

For a site that must continue independently, design and test the required local capabilities—not just a redundant network link.

  • Operate: local identity and policy permit the explicitly approved functions.
  • Defend: local staff can detect, triage and contain incidents without the central SOC.
  • Recover: independent keys, tools, people and an isolated recovery environment remain available.
  • Protect safety: review cyber response actions with local operators before they affect physical processes.
05 / Assurance

Test the paths that matter

Resilience is more than keeping a service online. Recovery must work even when the normal administration path is suspect.

Map authority

Find indirect privilege

Trace who can act through agents, automation and integrations; document credentials, permissions and revocation.

Break dependencies

Plan independent recovery

Keep versioned baselines, retained logs and recovery access outside the suspected compromise; test restoration in dependency order.

Exercise the design

Validate the real failure

Test lost identity, communications and security operations against required outage durations, safety constraints and recovery objectives.

Review in order: mandatory constraints → authority and dependencies → failure scenarios → risk, cost and complexity → operating capability. Reject designs that violate a mandatory requirement before comparing tradeoffs.

Confirm legal, safety and product requirements for each environment. Approve deployment only when scenario tests demonstrate required continuity and recovery behavior.
